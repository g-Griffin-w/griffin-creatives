THE 8 PHOTOS EVERY
OUTFITTER PAGE NEEDS.

A hunter cannot visit before they book, so your photos are the closest they get. The eight shots that answer their questions, and how to take them with a phone.

Why photos matter more than copy

A guided hunt is a big spend and the hunter cannot visit first. Your photos are the closest they get. They scroll your website or your Google profile and decide in a few seconds whether you look like a real, working operation. Paragraphs of text do not do that. Photos do.

You do not need a photographer. A phone, clean lens and good light cover almost all of it. What you need is the right eight subjects.

The 8 photos

1. A real hunter with an animal

Your own client, in your own camp country. Not a stock photo, and not only the biggest bull you have ever had. A range of real hunts tells a hunter what a normal week with you looks like. Ask the client before you post it.

2. You, the guide

A clear photo of your face. Hunters book people, and this is the first thing they look for. Add a short line under it about who you are and how long you have guided.

3. Camp from the outside

Wall tents, a cabin or a lodge, shot in daylight. It shows where they will be sleeping before they ever have to ask.

4. Inside: bunks and bedding

The photo that answers "is it comfortable?" without a phone call. Make the beds, clear the clutter and shoot from the doorway.

5. The food

A real meal on your camp table. Hunters ask about it constantly. Show them.

6. How you get in

Horses, a pack string, trucks or ATVs. It tells a hunter how hard the hunt is, and who it suits.

7. The country

The terrain you actually hunt. It sets expectations about the fitness a hunt takes and what a hunter will see each day.

8. The hunt in progress

Glassing a ridge, a pack-out, a camp morning with coffee. It shows the experience, not only the result.

Protect your spots

This is the part most outfitters miss. Photos can give away the places you work:

  • Crop landmarks. A distinctive peak, ridge line or lake in the background can identify a drainage.
  • Turn off location tagging. Most phones save the GPS location inside each photo. Social platforms usually strip it, but a file you upload to your own website can keep it. Switch location off in your camera settings, or remove the location data before you upload.

Where the photos go

  • Your website: a real photo on the first screen, then camp, food and country further down.
  • Your Google Business Profile: hunters often see these photos before they ever see your site. See the Google profile setup guide. Google recommends JPG or PNG files between 10 KB and 5 MB, at least 720 by 720 pixels, in focus and well lit. Check Google's current guidelines before you upload.
  • Your rebooking emails: one good camp photo in the thank-you email does more than a paragraph.

Small things that help the page

  • Name each file for what it shows, like hunter-with-bull-elk-camp.jpg, not IMG_4021.jpg.
  • Write a short description (alt text) for every image, such as "Hunter with a bull elk at camp". It helps people using screen readers and tells search engines what the picture shows.
  • Shrink large phone photos before you upload them so your page loads quickly on a phone.

Four photo mistakes

  • Stock photos of mountains that could be anyone's.
  • Only trophy shots, nothing about camp or the days before.
  • Old, blurry or sideways photos with no date.
  • Photos that give away your honey hole.

Get all eight on your next trip

  • Your phone is enough. Wipe the lens first.
  • Shoot in the first and last hour of daylight.
  • Ask every client before you post their photo.
  • Add them to your website and your Google profile.

Common questions

What photos should an outfitter have on their website?

Eight: a real hunter with an animal, the guide, camp from the outside, the inside of camp, the food, how you get in, the country you hunt and a hunt in progress. Together they show a hunter what the week actually looks like.

Do I need a professional photographer?

No. A phone with a clean lens, shot in the first and last hour of daylight, covers almost everything. Spend your effort on getting the right eight subjects.

How do I keep my hunting spots secret in photos?

Crop out distinctive landmarks, and turn off location tagging. Most phones save GPS data in each photo and a file uploaded to your own website can keep it, so switch location off in the camera settings or remove it before you upload.

Should I put the same photos on my Google profile?

Yes. Many hunters see your Google profile before they see your website, so add the same real photos there. Google recommends JPG or PNG files between 10 KB and 5 MB, at least 720 by 720 pixels.

Do I need permission to post a client's photo?

Ask every client before you post. Most are glad to say yes, and asking keeps the relationship good.
