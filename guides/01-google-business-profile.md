# Set Up Your Google Business Profile (the DIY version)

**Time:** about 45 minutes of work, plus Google's verification wait.
**Do it once, then spend 10 minutes a month keeping it fresh.**

## Why this is the highest-leverage hour you'll spend

A hunter planning a once-a-year trip doesn't call the first outfitter they hear about. They search. For a lot of local searches Google shows a map with three businesses above everything else, including your website, and those three listings get most of the calls. Your profile is also what shows up when someone searches your business name, so it's the first impression whether you set it up or not.

What a hunter is trying to answer in the 20 seconds they spend on your listing: *Is this a real operation? Where's camp? Who's guiding me? What does the country look like? Do guys actually kill elk here?* A filled-out profile answers all five before they ever click through.

## Step 1: Claim it (and check for duplicates first)

Search your business name on Google Maps. Also search your old business names and your phone number. Outfitters change names, add partners, and move, and it's common to find two or three stale listings for the same operation. Duplicates split your reviews and confuse Google about which one is real.

- If a listing exists: click **"Claim this business."**
- If you find duplicates: claim the real one, then use "Suggest an edit" on the others to mark them closed or duplicate.
- If nothing exists: go to business.google.com and add it.

## Step 2: Business name, exactly as it appears on your sign

Use your real name: **"Bear Paw Outfitters"**, not "Bear Paw Outfitters | Best Montana Elk Hunting Guide." Stuffing keywords into the name is against Google's guidelines and is one of the most common reasons listings get suspended.

## Step 3: Verify

Google will offer some mix of postcard, phone call, text, email, or video. Postcards can take a week or more, and they go to a physical address, so make sure someone will actually open the mail at that address. **Start verification first**, then finish the rest while you wait.

## Step 4: Service area, not a storefront

You don't have a storefront. Hunters don't drive to your ranch to browse. You're a **service-area business**, so:

- Hide your street address (it's a setting, and it matters if your camp is also your home).
- Add the regions you actually hunt: the counties, the national forest, the unit areas. Not just your mailing address town.

## Step 5: Pick your categories

Open the primary category dropdown and search "hunting," "outfitter," and "guide." Pick the closest match. Then do the check most people skip: search *"elk hunting outfitter [your region]"* and look at which category the top three results use. That's what Google is rewarding in your area. Add secondary categories only if they're true (for example, if you also run fishing trips).

## Step 6: Write the description

You get 750 characters. Put the important details first, because the preview cuts off. Use this structure:

> [Camp name] is a family-run elk outfitter guiding [fully guided / semi-guided] [archery and rifle] hunts in [region / units] since [year]. We hunt [public land / private ranch / wilderness] out of a [pack-in camp / lodge], with [number] hunters per week and [ratio] guides. Hunts include [lodging, meals, guide, pack-out, meat care]. Licenses, tags, and transport to camp are not included. Call or book online to reserve [year] dates.

Say what's included and what isn't. Hunters read that line twice.

## Step 7: List each hunt as its own service

Don't make one vague "Hunting" entry. Create a service for each real hunt, with a name, a short description, and a price or "from $X":

> **5-Day Guided Archery Elk Hunt, September**
> Fully guided, [number] hunters per guide, pack-in camp. Includes meals, lodging, guide, and meat care. From $[price].

## Step 8: Photos, with a shot list

Hunters want to see what they're buying. Upload 15 to 20 real photos covering:

1. **Cover photo:** the country with your camp or horses in frame
2. **Camp:** tents or cabins, and the kitchen
3. **Stock:** horses or mules loaded and on the trail
4. **Your guides:** at work, glassing, calling
5. **The hunt:** a glassing shot at dawn, a hunter and guide on a ridge
6. **Success shots:** bull down with the hunter (always get permission)
7. **Food and fire:** the evening in camp

No stock photos, ever. Add a few new ones each month through the season.

## Step 9: Contact details that match everywhere

Phone, website, and business name should read the **same way** on your profile, your website, Facebook, and anywhere else you're listed. If you're in the backcountry and can't pick up, say so in the description and set up a voicemail that promises a callback within a day.

## Step 10: Keep it alive

- **Post** when you have openings, season updates, or a fresh photo. Two lines is fine.
- **Reply to every review** within a couple of days (see the reviews guide).
- **Update seasonal details** each January: dates, prices, availability.

A profile with recent activity looks alive to Google and to hunters. One that sat untouched for two years looks abandoned.

## The mistakes that cost outfitters the most

- Keyword-stuffed business name (suspension risk)
- Leaving your home address visible when it's also where you live
- Claiming categories that aren't true
- Using stock photos or brochure shots instead of real ones
- Never replying to reviews
- Letting phone numbers differ between your site and your profile
